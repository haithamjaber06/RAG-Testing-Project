THE PRACTICAL GYM AND HEALTH HANDBOOK

A beginner-friendly guide to training, nutrition, recovery and staying healthy, followed by the member handbook of Iron Haven Fitness, a fictional gym.


PART 1: INTRODUCTION

Most people who join a gym quit within the first few months. The reason is rarely laziness. Usually they start with no plan, copy a routine built for an advanced athlete, get sore or injured, see little progress, and conclude that the gym is not for them. This handbook exists to prevent that. It explains the principles behind training and nutrition in plain language, so that you can make your own decisions instead of following random advice from social media.

The good news is that the fundamentals of fitness have not changed much in decades. Lift weights with good technique, add a little more over time, move your body most days, eat enough protein, sleep well, and be patient. Almost everything else is detail. The details do matter once the basics are in place, and this guide covers many of them, but if you remember only one thing, remember that consistency over months beats intensity over days. A moderate program you follow for a year will always beat a perfect program you abandon after three weeks.

This handbook is not medical advice. If you have a heart condition, high blood pressure, diabetes, a recent injury, are pregnant, or have any other medical concern, speak to a doctor before starting a new exercise program.


PART 2: HOW MUSCLES GROW

Muscle growth, called hypertrophy, happens when the body adapts to a demand it is not used to. When you lift a challenging weight, your muscle fibres experience high mechanical tension. This tension is the main signal that tells the body to build more muscle protein. Over the following hours and days, if you have eaten enough protein and rested, the body repairs and reinforces the muscle so that it is better prepared for the same demand next time.

For a long time people believed that muscle damage, the microscopic tears that cause soreness, was the main driver of growth. Current research suggests that damage is not required for growth and that too much of it can actually slow progress, because the body spends its resources on repair rather than on building new tissue. Soreness is therefore not a reliable sign of a good workout. You can have an excellent session and feel almost nothing the next day.

The key principle that keeps muscles growing is progressive overload. Once your body has adapted to a certain weight, that weight is no longer a new demand, and growth slows down. To keep progressing, you must gradually increase the challenge. The most common way is to add weight to the bar, but there are others: performing more repetitions with the same weight, adding an extra set, slowing down the lowering phase of a lift, reducing rest time, or improving your range of motion. A beginner can often add weight to the main lifts every week for several months. An advanced lifter may only add a small amount every few months.

Beginners experience something often called newbie gains. Because the body has never been exposed to resistance training, it responds very quickly, and a new lifter can gain strength and muscle far faster than someone with years of experience. Much of the strength gain in the first six to eight weeks comes from the nervous system learning to recruit muscle fibres more efficiently, not from the muscles getting bigger. This is why beginners often get much stronger before they look any different in the mirror.

Muscle cannot be turned into fat, and fat cannot be turned into muscle. They are completely different tissues. When people say they want to "convert fat into muscle", what actually happens is that they lose fat through a calorie deficit and build muscle through training, and the two processes can happen at the same time, especially in beginners. This is called body recomposition.

Genetics affect how quickly you build muscle, how your muscles are shaped, and where your body stores fat. You cannot change your genetics, but nearly everyone can make large improvements in strength and appearance with consistent training. Comparing yourself to people online, many of whom have exceptional genetics, years of training, or use performance-enhancing drugs, is one of the fastest ways to lose motivation.


PART 3: STRENGTH, HYPERTROPHY AND ENDURANCE

The number of repetitions you perform per set changes what kind of adaptation you get, although the differences are smaller than many people think.

Heavy sets of 1 to 5 repetitions are the most effective for building maximal strength, meaning the most weight you can lift once. This range trains the nervous system to produce as much force as possible and is the foundation of powerlifting programs.

Moderate sets of roughly 6 to 12 repetitions are traditionally called the hypertrophy range. Research over the last decade has shown that muscle growth is actually similar across a wide range, from about 5 to 30 repetitions, as long as each set is taken close to failure. The moderate range remains popular because it is efficient: the weights are heavy enough to be challenging but light enough to be safe and not too exhausting.

Light sets of 15 or more repetitions build muscular endurance, the ability to keep working for longer. They can still build muscle, but they are very tiring and take longer to perform.

How close you train to failure matters more than the exact number of repetitions. Failure means the point where you cannot complete another repetition with good form. Two useful tools for judging effort are RPE and RIR. RPE stands for Rating of Perceived Exertion and is usually scored from 1 to 10, where 10 means you could not have done another repetition. RIR stands for Reps in Reserve, the number of repetitions you think you could still have done. An RPE of 8 is roughly equal to 2 RIR. For most sets, aiming for 1 to 3 reps in reserve gives excellent results without the extra fatigue and injury risk of going to true failure on every set. Training to complete failure is not recommended on heavy compound lifts such as squats and deadlifts, where form tends to break down dangerously.

Rest periods also matter. For heavy compound lifts, resting 2 to 5 minutes between sets allows you to recover enough to lift well on the next set. For smaller isolation exercises, 1 to 2 minutes is usually enough. Resting too little forces you to use lighter weights, which reduces the training stimulus.


PART 4: DESIGNING A TRAINING PROGRAM

A training program answers four questions: how often you train each muscle, how much work you do, which exercises you perform, and how you progress over time.

Frequency. Training each muscle group about twice per week works better for most people than training it once per week. This is why many popular programs use full-body sessions or an upper/lower split rather than the traditional "bro split", in which each muscle is trained once per week on its own day, such as chest on Monday and back on Tuesday. The bro split is not useless, but it usually means doing a lot of work for one muscle in a single session and then leaving it alone for a whole week.

Volume. Training volume is usually counted as the number of hard sets per muscle group per week. A hard set is one taken within about 3 repetitions of failure. Most research suggests that roughly 10 to 20 hard sets per muscle group per week works well for muscle growth. Beginners can grow very well with less, often 6 to 10 sets, and doing far more than 20 sets usually adds fatigue without adding much growth.

Common program structures include:

Full body, three days per week. Every session trains the whole body, typically with one exercise for the lower body, one push, one pull, and some accessory work. This is the best structure for most beginners because it trains every muscle three times per week, it is simple, and missing one session does not leave a muscle untrained for a week.

Upper/lower, four days per week. Two days train the upper body and two days train the lower body. This allows a little more volume per session and suits people who have trained for six months to a year.

Push/pull/legs, three or six days per week. Push days train chest, shoulders and triceps; pull days train back and biceps; leg days train the lower body. Run six days per week, it trains everything twice. It is popular with intermediate lifters but demands a lot of time.

Exercise selection. A good program is built around compound exercises, which work several joints and muscles at once, such as squats, deadlifts, bench presses, overhead presses, rows and pull-ups. Isolation exercises, such as bicep curls, lateral raises and leg extensions, train a single muscle and are useful additions, but they should not form the core of a beginner's program.

Progression. The simplest progression model for beginners is linear progression: add a small amount of weight each session or each week. A common approach is to add 2.5 kg to upper-body lifts and 5 kg to lower-body lifts whenever you complete all your target repetitions. When progress stalls, you can switch to double progression: choose a repetition range such as 8 to 12, keep the weight the same until you can perform 12 repetitions on all sets, then increase the weight and start again at 8.

Deloads. A deload is a planned week of easier training, usually with about half the normal volume or lighter weights. Intermediate and advanced lifters often deload every 4 to 8 weeks to let accumulated fatigue disappear. Beginners rarely need scheduled deloads, but should take one if they feel run down, their performance drops for two sessions in a row, or their joints ache.

A sample beginner full-body program, performed on Monday, Wednesday and Friday:

Day A: Squat 3 sets of 5, Bench press 3 sets of 5, Barbell row 3 sets of 8, Plank 3 sets of 30 seconds.
Day B: Deadlift 1 set of 5 after warm-ups, Overhead press 3 sets of 5, Lat pulldown or pull-ups 3 sets of 8, Dumbbell lunges 2 sets of 10 per leg.

Alternate the two days: A, B, A in the first week, then B, A, B in the second week.


PART 5: THE MAIN COMPOUND LIFTS

The squat. Stand with your feet about shoulder-width apart and your toes turned slightly outward. Take a deep breath into your belly, brace your core as if someone were about to punch you in the stomach, and sit down between your heels by bending your hips and knees at the same time. Keep your chest up and your whole foot on the floor, with your weight balanced over the middle of the foot. Your knees should travel in the same direction as your toes, and it is completely normal and safe for them to move forward past your toes. The old rule that knees must never pass the toes is a myth; for most people, forcing the knees back makes the movement harder on the lower back. Go as deep as you can while keeping your lower back neutral, ideally until your hip crease is below the top of your knee, then drive back up.

The deadlift. Stand with the bar over the middle of your feet, about where your shoelaces are tied. Bend down and grip the bar just outside your legs. Push your hips back, bring your shins forward until they touch the bar, and pull your chest up so your back is flat. Before lifting, pull the slack out of the bar by tensing your arms and back until you hear the plates click against the bar. Then push the floor away with your legs, keeping the bar in contact with your legs the whole way up, and finish by standing tall without leaning backwards. The most common mistake is rounding the lower back under heavy load. A deadlift should not be performed with a rounded lower back when the weight is heavy, and if your form breaks down, the weight is too heavy for now.

The bench press. Lie on the bench with your eyes directly under the bar. Grip the bar slightly wider than shoulder-width, squeeze your shoulder blades together and down, and keep your feet flat on the floor. Lower the bar under control to your lower chest, with your elbows at roughly 45 to 70 degrees from your body rather than flared straight out to the sides, then press it back up. Always use a spotter or the safety arms of a power rack when benching heavy. Never bench alone with heavy weight and no safeties, because if you fail a repetition the bar can trap you.

The overhead press. Stand with the bar resting on your front shoulders and your grip just outside shoulder-width. Squeeze your glutes and brace your core so you do not lean back. Press the bar straight up, moving your head slightly back to let the bar pass your face, then bring your head forward under the bar at the top. This lift develops the shoulders and triceps and builds stability throughout the whole body.

The barbell row. Hinge forward at the hips until your torso is roughly 45 degrees or lower, with a flat back and slightly bent knees. Pull the bar towards your lower ribs, leading with your elbows, and lower it under control. Avoid using momentum by jerking your torso up and down; if you have to swing, the weight is too heavy.

The pull-up. Hang from a bar with your palms facing away and your hands slightly wider than your shoulders. Pull your chest towards the bar until your chin clears it, then lower yourself all the way down. Many beginners cannot yet perform a single pull-up. Good ways to build up to one include the lat pulldown machine, band-assisted pull-ups, and negatives, where you jump to the top position and lower yourself as slowly as possible.

Breathing and bracing. On heavy lifts, take a big breath before each repetition and hold it while you brace your core, then breathe out at the top. This technique, a controlled version of the Valsalva manoeuvre, stabilises the spine. Because it temporarily raises blood pressure, people with uncontrolled high blood pressure or heart conditions should speak to a doctor before using it.

Lifting belts. A belt gives your core something to push against and can help you brace on very heavy sets. Beginners do not need a belt; it is more useful to first learn to brace without one.


PART 6: WARMING UP AND MOBILITY

A good warm-up raises your body temperature, increases blood flow to the muscles, and lets you rehearse the movement before the heavy sets. Start with 5 minutes of light cardio such as cycling or brisk walking, then perform a few dynamic movements such as leg swings, arm circles and bodyweight squats.

The most important part of the warm-up is the ramp-up sets. Before your working sets of a lift, perform a few sets with gradually increasing weight. For example, if your working weight for squats is 80 kg, you might do 10 repetitions with the empty bar, 5 with 40 kg, 3 with 60 kg, and 1 or 2 with 70 kg before starting your working sets.

Long static stretches, where you hold a stretch for 60 seconds or more, are not recommended immediately before heavy lifting, because they can temporarily reduce strength and power. Short stretches of under 30 seconds have little negative effect. Static stretching is better saved for after the workout or for a separate session.

Mobility refers to your ability to move a joint actively through its full range. If you cannot reach a good squat depth, the cause is often limited ankle mobility, tight hips, or simply a lack of practice. Squatting regularly through your full available range is itself one of the best ways to improve squat mobility. Raising your heels slightly, either with weightlifting shoes or small plates under your heels, is a legitimate way to squat deeper while your mobility improves.


PART 7: CARDIOVASCULAR TRAINING

Strength training builds muscle and bone, but it does not replace cardiovascular exercise, which strengthens the heart and lungs and is strongly linked to a longer life. The World Health Organization recommends that adults do at least 150 to 300 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic activity per week, or 75 to 150 minutes of vigorous activity, together with muscle-strengthening activities on two or more days per week.

Moderate intensity means you can talk but not sing. Brisk walking, easy cycling and light jogging usually fall into this category. Vigorous intensity means you can only say a few words before needing to breathe. Running, fast cycling and swimming laps are typical examples.

Zone 2 training has become popular in recent years. It refers to steady exercise at a low to moderate intensity, often defined as roughly 60 to 70 percent of your maximum heart rate, where you can still hold a conversation. Zone 2 builds your aerobic base, improves the ability of your muscles to use fat as fuel, and is easy to recover from, so it combines well with lifting.

High-intensity interval training, or HIIT, alternates short bursts of very hard effort with periods of rest or easy movement, for example 30 seconds of sprinting followed by 90 seconds of walking, repeated 8 times. HIIT is time-efficient and improves fitness quickly, but it is demanding and harder to recover from. Doing HIIT every day is not recommended; two sessions per week is plenty for most people, especially if they also lift weights.

A common way to estimate maximum heart rate is to subtract your age from 220. This formula is only a rough estimate and can be wrong by 10 to 20 beats per minute for an individual, so treat it as a starting point and pay attention to how hard the effort actually feels.

Does cardio kill your gains? Moderate amounts of cardio do not meaningfully reduce muscle growth for most people. Very large amounts, especially of running, can interfere somewhat with strength and size gains, an effect sometimes called the interference effect. To minimise it, separate cardio and lifting by several hours when possible, or do cardio after lifting rather than before, and prefer cycling or walking over long runs if building muscle is your main goal.

Daily movement outside the gym also matters a great deal. Non-exercise activity thermogenesis, or NEAT, is the energy you burn through everyday activity such as walking, standing and doing chores. It can vary by several hundred calories per day between people. Aiming for around 7,000 to 10,000 steps per day is a simple, practical target for most adults.


PART 8: NUTRITION

Energy balance. Your body weight is mostly determined by energy balance: the calories you eat compared with the calories you burn. Eating more than you burn, a calorie surplus, leads to weight gain. Eating less than you burn, a calorie deficit, leads to weight loss. Food quality matters enormously for health, satiety and performance, but no specific food or diet can override energy balance.

For fat loss, a moderate deficit of around 300 to 500 calories per day below maintenance is usually sustainable and helps preserve muscle. A reasonable rate of weight loss for most people is about 0.5 to 1 percent of body weight per week. Losing weight much faster than this increases muscle loss, hunger and the chance of regaining the weight. Crash diets of very low calories are not recommended.

For muscle gain, a small surplus of around 200 to 300 calories per day above maintenance supports growth while limiting fat gain. A beginner might aim to gain about 0.25 to 0.5 kg per month; gaining faster than that usually means adding mostly fat.

Protein. Protein provides the amino acids that muscles are built from. For people who lift weights, a daily intake of about 1.6 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight captures most of the benefit for muscle growth, and some people go up to about 2.2 grams per kilogram, particularly when dieting, to help preserve muscle. For a 70 kg person, 1.6 g/kg is about 112 grams of protein per day. Spreading protein across 3 to 5 meals, each containing roughly 20 to 40 grams, is a practical approach. Good sources include chicken, fish, eggs, Greek yogurt, lean beef, lentils, beans, tofu and milk.

High protein intakes are safe for healthy people. The claim that high-protein diets damage healthy kidneys is not supported by research, although people with existing kidney disease should follow their doctor's advice on protein.

Carbohydrates. Carbohydrates are the body's preferred fuel for intense exercise. They are stored in the muscles and liver as glycogen. People who train hard generally perform better with a reasonable carbohydrate intake. Good sources include rice, oats, potatoes, bread, pasta, fruit and vegetables. Carbohydrates do not make you fat on their own; excess calories do, whatever their source.

Fats. Dietary fat is essential for hormone production, the absorption of vitamins A, D, E and K, and overall health. Fats should usually make up about 20 to 35 percent of total calories. Good sources include olive oil, nuts, seeds, avocados and oily fish such as salmon and sardines. Very low-fat diets are not recommended because they can disrupt hormone levels.

Fibre, fruit and vegetables. Aim to fill about half your plate with vegetables and fruit at most meals. Fibre improves digestion, helps control appetite and supports heart health. Most adults benefit from around 25 to 35 grams of fibre per day.

Hydration. Water needs vary with body size, climate and activity. A simple guide is to drink enough that your urine is pale yellow. During long or very sweaty sessions, especially in hot weather, replace both fluid and sodium.

Meal timing. Total daily intake matters far more than precise timing. The idea of a strict 30-minute "anabolic window" after training, in which you must drink a protein shake or lose your gains, is greatly exaggerated. Eating a meal containing protein within a few hours before or after training is sufficient for nearly everyone.

Alcohol. Alcohol contains about 7 calories per gram, impairs sleep quality, and in larger amounts reduces muscle protein synthesis and recovery. Occasional light drinking will not ruin your progress, but heavy drinking, especially after training, works against your goals.


PART 9: SUPPLEMENTS

Supplements are, as the name suggests, supplementary. They cannot make up for poor training, poor eating or poor sleep. Most of the supplement industry sells products with little or no evidence behind them. A few, however, are well supported by research.

Creatine monohydrate. Creatine is the most researched sports supplement in the world. It increases the stores of phosphocreatine in your muscles, which helps regenerate energy during short, intense efforts such as lifting. Taking 3 to 5 grams per day consistently improves strength, power and muscle gain over time. You can optionally "load" with about 20 grams per day split into four doses for 5 to 7 days to saturate your muscles faster, but loading is not necessary; taking 3 to 5 grams daily reaches the same level after about 3 to 4 weeks. Timing does not matter much. Creatine often causes an initial weight gain of 1 to 2 kg, which is water stored inside the muscles, not fat. In healthy people creatine is safe, and it does not damage the kidneys or liver. Cycling creatine on and off is not necessary. Plain creatine monohydrate is as effective as more expensive forms.

Caffeine. Caffeine improves alertness, endurance and strength performance. An effective dose is about 3 to 6 milligrams per kilogram of body weight, taken 30 to 60 minutes before training. Many pre-workout products are mostly caffeine. Caffeine has a half-life of around 5 hours, so taking it late in the day can disrupt sleep. Avoid caffeine within about 8 hours of bedtime if you are sensitive to it.

Protein powder. Whey protein is simply a convenient, cheap source of high-quality protein derived from milk. It is not magic and offers no advantage over the same amount of protein from food, but it makes reaching your protein target easier. Plant-based protein powders made from pea, rice or soy are good alternatives for people who avoid dairy.

Vitamin D. Many people, especially those who spend little time outdoors or live far from the equator, have low vitamin D levels. Vitamin D supports bone health and immune function. If you suspect a deficiency, a blood test is the best way to find out before supplementing at high doses.

Omega-3 fatty acids. Fish oil supplements provide EPA and DHA, which support heart health. If you rarely eat oily fish, a fish oil supplement can be worthwhile.

Supplements with weak or no evidence for muscle growth in people who already eat enough protein include BCAAs (branched-chain amino acids), testosterone boosters, fat burners, glutamine and most proprietary "blends". BCAAs are unnecessary if your daily protein intake is adequate, because protein foods already contain them.

Supplements are not tightly regulated in many countries, and some products have been found to contain undeclared stimulants or banned substances. Choose products that are third-party tested, especially if you compete in a tested sport.


PART 10: SLEEP AND RECOVERY

Muscles do not grow during the workout; they grow during recovery. Sleep is the most powerful recovery tool available, and it is free. Adults should aim for 7 to 9 hours of sleep per night. Research shows that sleeping too little reduces muscle protein synthesis, increases the proportion of weight lost as muscle during a diet, raises hunger hormones, lowers testosterone, and impairs strength, focus and motivation.

To improve sleep, keep a consistent bedtime and wake time, even on weekends. Keep the bedroom dark, quiet and cool, around 18 to 20 degrees Celsius. Avoid caffeine in the afternoon and evening, limit alcohol, and reduce bright screen use in the hour before bed. A short walk outdoors in the morning light helps set your body clock.

Rest days are part of training, not a break from it. Most beginners do best with at least one or two full rest days per week. Active recovery, such as walking, light cycling or gentle mobility work, can help you feel less stiff without adding fatigue.

Delayed onset muscle soreness, or DOMS, is the muscle soreness that appears after unfamiliar or hard exercise. It typically begins 12 to 24 hours after training, peaks between 24 and 72 hours, and fades within about 5 days. DOMS is normal, especially when starting a new program, and it decreases as your body adapts. Light movement helps relieve it. You do not need to wait until soreness has completely disappeared before training a muscle again, but very severe soreness is a sign you did too much too soon.

Overtraining syndrome, a long-term drop in performance with persistent fatigue, mood changes and poor sleep, is actually rare in recreational lifters. Much more common is simple under-recovery caused by poor sleep, poor nutrition and life stress. Before blaming your program, check your sleep and your food.

A rare but serious condition called rhabdomyolysis can occur after extremely intense exercise, especially in untrained people doing far too much too soon. Muscle tissue breaks down rapidly and releases proteins into the blood that can damage the kidneys. Warning signs include extreme muscle pain and swelling, weakness, and dark brown or cola-coloured urine. This is a medical emergency; anyone with these symptoms should seek medical care immediately.


PART 11: INJURIES AND PAIN

Strength training is one of the safest forms of exercise, with lower injury rates than most team sports. Injuries usually come from lifting too heavy too soon, using poor form, or ignoring warning signs.

It is important to distinguish between normal training discomfort and pain that signals injury. The burning feeling in a muscle during a hard set, and general muscle soreness in the following days, are normal. Sharp, stabbing or sudden pain, pain in a joint rather than a muscle, pain that gets worse as you continue, or pain accompanied by swelling, numbness or tingling are warning signs. If you feel any of these, stop the exercise.

For many years the standard advice for acute injuries was RICE: Rest, Ice, Compression and Elevation. Advice has since evolved. Complete rest is now considered less helpful than early, gentle movement within the limits of pain, and the benefit of ice beyond short-term pain relief is uncertain. Newer frameworks such as PEACE and LOVE, published in the British Journal of Sports Medicine in 2019, emphasise protecting the area for the first few days, avoiding anti-inflammatory medication in the very early stage, educating the patient, and then gradually loading the injured tissue and returning to exercise.

Common gym injuries include lower back strains, often from rounding the back during deadlifts or lifting with poor bracing; shoulder pain, frequently linked to benching with the elbows flared straight out; knee pain, sometimes from sudden increases in squat volume; and elbow tendon pain, often caused by too much gripping and curling.

Many minor aches can be managed by temporarily reducing the weight or volume, changing the exercise to a pain-free variation, and building back up gradually. Pain does not always mean damage, and complete inactivity often makes things worse. However, you should see a doctor or physiotherapist if pain lasts more than two weeks, gets steadily worse, wakes you up at night, or follows a clear injury such as a pop or sudden giving way of a joint. Seek urgent medical help for chest pain, severe shortness of breath, fainting, or numbness in the groin area with back pain.


PART 12: COMMON BEGINNER MISTAKES

Ego lifting. Lifting more weight than you can handle with good form, usually to impress others, is the fastest route to injury. Nobody in the gym cares how much you lift as much as you think they do.

Program hopping. Switching routines every two weeks prevents progressive overload. Choose a sensible program and run it for at least 8 to 12 weeks before judging it.

Skipping legs. Training only the chest and arms leads to an unbalanced physique and misses the largest muscles in the body, which burn the most energy and support everyday movement.

Only doing cardio to lose fat. Cardio burns calories, but strength training preserves and builds muscle while you diet, which changes your body shape far more than cardio alone. The most effective approach combines both with a moderate calorie deficit.

Trying to spot-reduce fat. Doing hundreds of crunches will strengthen your abdominal muscles but will not burn the fat covering them. You cannot choose where your body loses fat; it comes off according to your genetics as your overall body fat decreases.

Neglecting sleep and nutrition. Training provides the stimulus; food and sleep provide the materials and the time to adapt. Without them, even the best program produces poor results.

Not tracking anything. If you do not write down what you lifted, you cannot know whether you are progressing, and you cannot apply progressive overload reliably.

Expecting fast results. Visible changes typically take 8 to 12 weeks of consistent training and eating. Strength usually improves much sooner, so use your logbook as your early source of motivation.


PART 13: TRACKING PROGRESS

Keep a training log, either in a notebook or an app, recording the exercise, weight, sets and repetitions for every session. Over months, this log becomes the most reliable evidence that your training is working.

The bathroom scale is a useful tool but a noisy one. Daily body weight can fluctuate by 1 to 2 kg because of water, salt, carbohydrate intake, digestion and hormones. Weigh yourself in the morning after using the toilet and before eating, several times per week, and look at the weekly average rather than any single reading.

Because the scale cannot tell muscle from fat, combine it with other measures. Take progress photos every 4 weeks in the same lighting and pose, and measure your waist at the level of your navel. A shrinking waist with stable body weight usually means you are losing fat and gaining muscle.

Body fat scales that send a small electrical current through your feet, known as bioelectrical impedance devices, are convenient but often inaccurate for individuals and strongly affected by hydration. Use them, if at all, only to watch long-term trends.

Body mass index, or BMI, is calculated by dividing your weight in kilograms by the square of your height in metres. It is useful for studying populations but limited for individuals, because it does not distinguish muscle from fat. A muscular lifter can be classified as "overweight" by BMI while having low body fat.


PART 14: IRON HAVEN FITNESS MEMBER HANDBOOK (FICTIONAL GYM)

The following section is the member handbook of Iron Haven Fitness, a fictional gym used as an example in this document.

Opening hours. Iron Haven Fitness is open from 5:30 AM to 11:00 PM Monday to Thursday, 5:30 AM to 10:00 PM on Friday, and 7:00 AM to 8:00 PM on Saturday and Sunday. The gym is closed on the first day of each year for deep cleaning. Members on the Titan plan have 24-hour access using their key card.

Membership plans. Iron Haven offers three plans. The Bronze plan costs 25 JOD per month and includes access to the main gym floor during staffed hours. The Silver plan costs 40 JOD per month and adds group classes, the sauna, and one free personal training session per month. The Titan plan costs 60 JOD per month and adds 24-hour access, a private locker, towel service, and four free personal training sessions per month. Students with a valid university ID receive a 20 percent discount on the Bronze and Silver plans, but the student discount does not apply to the Titan plan.

Joining fee and contracts. New members pay a one-time joining fee of 15 JOD, which is waived during the January and September promotions. All plans require a minimum commitment of 3 months. After that, membership continues month to month and can be cancelled with 30 days' written notice using form IH-C2 at the front desk or in the Iron Haven app.

Freezing your membership. Members can freeze their membership for a minimum of 2 weeks and a maximum of 3 months per calendar year, for example during travel, exams or illness. Freezes must be requested at least 7 days in advance using form IH-F12. A medical freeze can be requested at any time with a doctor's note and does not count towards the 3-month limit. Membership fees are not charged during a freeze, but a 5 JOD administration fee applies to each non-medical freeze.

Guest policy. Silver and Titan members may bring one guest per visit, up to four times per month. Bronze members may not bring guests. Each guest pays a day-pass fee of 7 JOD, must be at least 16 years old, and must sign the liability waiver at reception before entering.

Age requirements. Members must be at least 16 years old. Members aged 16 and 17 need a signed parental consent form and may not use the free-weight area without completing an induction session with a staff trainer.

Lockers. Day lockers are free and must be emptied by closing time. Any items left in day lockers overnight are removed and kept at reception for 14 days, after which they are donated. Private lockers are included in the Titan plan and can be rented by other members for 8 JOD per month.

Gym floor rules. Re-rack all weights and return dumbbells to the correct place on the rack after use. Wipe down benches and machines after each use with the spray provided. Do not drop dumbbells from height; controlled drops of barbells are allowed only on the lifting platforms. Chalk is permitted only on the lifting platforms. Do not rest between sets on a machine for longer than necessary during busy hours, and allow others to "work in" with you. Filming is allowed for your own training, but you must not film other members without their permission. Outdoor shoes are not allowed on the gym floor, and closed-toe athletic shoes must be worn at all times.

Peak hours. The busiest times are 5:00 PM to 8:00 PM on weekdays. During peak hours, cardio machines have a 30-minute time limit when other members are waiting, and squat racks may not be used for bicep curls.

Personal training. Personal training sessions last 60 minutes. Additional sessions beyond those included in your plan cost 20 JOD each or 150 JOD for a package of 10. Sessions cancelled with less than 24 hours' notice are charged in full.

The Iron Haven app. Members use the Iron Haven app to book classes, request freezes, and view their visit history. Common app error codes include IH-401, which means your login session has expired and you must sign in again; IH-403, which means your membership is frozen or expired and booking is blocked; and IH-409, which means the class you tried to book is already full. If you see error IH-500, the booking system is temporarily down, and you should try again later or contact reception.

Safety. Staff trainers hold first-aid certification, and an automated external defibrillator (AED) is located next to the reception desk. Report any injury, broken equipment or spillage to staff immediately. Members must not train while ill with a fever or a contagious infection.


PART 15: FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS

How long until I see results? Most people notice strength improvements within 2 to 4 weeks and visible changes in their body within 8 to 12 weeks of consistent training and sensible eating.

How long should a workout last? A beginner workout can be completed in 45 to 75 minutes. Longer sessions are not automatically better; the quality and consistency of your training matter more than its duration.

Should I lift weights or do cardio first? If your main goal is strength or muscle, lift first while you are fresh, then do cardio. If your main goal is endurance, do the opposite.

Will lifting weights make women bulky? No. Building large amounts of muscle takes years of dedicated training and eating in a surplus, and women typically have much lower testosterone levels than men. For most women, lifting weights produces a firmer, stronger and more athletic look.

Can I train if I am sore? Usually yes. Mild soreness is not a reason to skip training, and a light session often reduces it. If soreness is severe, train a different muscle group or rest.

Is it bad to work out every day? Moving every day is excellent, but lifting hard with the same muscles every day without rest leaves no time for recovery. Alternate muscle groups and intensities, and include easier days.

Do I need to eat immediately after training? No. Eating a protein-containing meal within a few hours of training is enough.

Is it too late to start in my 40s, 50s or later? It is never too late. Older adults respond well to strength training and benefit greatly from it, because it slows the age-related loss of muscle and bone, improves balance, and lowers the risk of falls. Start with lighter weights and progress gradually, and check with a doctor first if you have medical conditions.
